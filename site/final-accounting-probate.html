Final Accounting in Probate: The Charge-and-Discharge Statement

At the end of administration the executor owes the court a final accounting: what came into the estate, what went out, and what remains. Most US courts expect it in a charge-and-discharge structure — the same shape for CA, NY and the states that accept a standard form.

The two columns every court looks for

CHARGESCREDITS
Inventory of assets at date of deathDisbursements (debts, expenses, fees)
Receipts during administrationLosses on sales
Gains on sales (over the date-of-death value)Distributions to beneficiaries
Property on hand at the end

TOTAL CHARGES must equal TOTAL CREDITS. Every dollar that came in is accounted for by a payment, a distribution, or an asset still held. When the two totals do not match, the difference tells you exactly what record is missing.

The three schedules attached to it

  1. Schedule of Receipts — each receipt: date, amount, source, description
  2. Schedule of Disbursements — each payment: date, payee, purpose, amount
  3. Schedule of Distributions — what went to each beneficiary, when

Disbursements generally need voucher support: cancelled checks or receipts. That is the part accountants call "tie-out", and it happens outside any software — the tool just keeps your ledger clean enough to tie out.

Sales: where gains and losses come from

When an inventoried asset sells, proceeds minus its date-of-death value is a gain (or loss) on sale — and the proceeds become estate cash. Recording the sale as ordinary income would double-count it; the correct pattern is one cash receipt plus a gain/loss line, which is how the Estate Ledger records it.

What this tool does — and does not

The Estate Ledger organizes the records and assembles a balanced statement you can hand your attorney or the court. Some courts mandate their own form or require the account to be sworn before a notary or the clerk — that filing step stays yours. This is an organizing tool, not legal advice.
